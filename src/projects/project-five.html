AmazeAmazeAmaze and Buddy

Spring 2026 · MIT 2.007 Design and Manufacturing I

Summary: Designed and built a robot from scratch for “F.007: Design to Survive,” the 2.007 Design and Manufacturing I competition, a Formula 1 themed game where two robots compete head-to-head on a shared race-themed table. This page follows the project from first concepts and hand calculations through prototyping, fabrication and testing, to a 704–675 match win.

Skills

  • Mechanism design: chain and sprocket transmission, gearing, winch-driven lift
  • Sheet-metal fabrication and assembly
  • Embedded electronics: motors, servos, battery, microcontroller, radio control
  • Iterative prototyping and testing

Strategy and early prototypes

Mini bot, then Buddy. Before starting the main robot, I built a small mini bot as a warm-up for designing it. It began as a simple test chassis to work out the drive layout, then moved to a labeled prototype on a folded sheet-metal body. That warm-up turned into Buddy, my small 3D-printed robot that does laps and paired with the main robot, AmazeAmazeAmaze, at competition.

Concept on paper. Before any metal was cut for AmazeAmazeAmaze, I mapped the game's scoring actions against how hard each would be to build, to find a strategy that scored well without betting everything on the hardest mechanisms. Then I sketched the spin-wheel module by hand — bearings, spacers, sprockets and motor mounts — to work out how the pieces would stack on a shaft.

Building the robot

The final chassis uses sheet-metal side walls and a base, cut, bent and fastened together, carrying a microcontroller, battery and radio receiver wired to the drive motors and servos.

Mechanisms

The spinner

One scoring action spins a wheel on the table: two motors drive a 1:2 gear ratio transmission through a sprocket and chain, turning a Delrin wheel insert. I modeled it in CAD, then built it.

The winch

A second scoring action pulls a tire in with a carabiner, winched back by a separate geared motor pair, sized to output at least 3 N·m of torque.

Attachment iteration

I built several small carabiner-style bracket plates with fasteners and a latch element, and tested them against the rails and the wheel on the competition table, iterating between versions.

An idea that didn't work

Early on, I prototyped a 3D-printed ratchet-and-pawl mechanism, meant to let the winch hold tension and lock in one direction without the motor fighting the load. It was a good idea on paper, but in practice the pawl didn't catch reliably under load, so I dropped it in favor of the simpler geared winch above. It's still one of the more important parts of the process — it's the iteration that told me what wouldn't work.
